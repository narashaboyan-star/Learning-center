School preparation

How to tell whether a child is ready for school—without tests or pressure

School readiness is not limited to reading, writing or counting. Communication, curiosity, independence, social skills and the ability to follow a familiar sequence also support a child's transition to school.

For parents of children approaching school age

The short answer: look at the whole picture, not one skill

The American Academy of Pediatrics describes school readiness as broader than early knowledge of letters, numbers and shapes. Social, emotional, behavioural, language and physical skills matter too. Children develop at different rates, so a single test or checklist cannot fully describe an individual child's readiness.

Important

This article cannot decide when a child should start school. It does not replace local school requirements, a teacher's observations or professional advice. Its purpose is to help parents notice strengths and areas where calm support may be useful.

1. Communication and emotional adjustment

Notice whether your child can ask an adult for help, wait for a turn in a familiar game, hear “no” and gradually return to an activity after disappointment. Perfect behaviour is not expected. What matters is that the child is learning to communicate a need and accept calm support.

2. Speech and understanding

In everyday situations, observe whether your child can follow a short explanation, retell a familiar story in their own words, ask a clarifying question and say what they did not understand. If familiar adults and other people regularly struggle to understand the child's speech, or ordinary instructions are consistently difficult to understand, discuss this with a qualified professional.

3. Attention, rules and sequences

At school, children need to hold a simple rule in mind, begin a task and move to the next step. These abilities are part of executive function and develop gradually with practice and adult support. You can observe them during rule-based games, packing belongings, preparing a simple snack together or completing two or three familiar actions in order.

4. Curiosity and early learning

Preparation does not have to mean pages of worksheets. Talking about a story, finding similar objects, sorting, counting in everyday situations, building and drawing help children notice relationships, express ideas and try solutions. UNICEF describes play and active exploration as important ways for young children to gain knowledge and skills.

5. Independence and movement

Look at how your child manages familiar routines: putting materials away, opening a container, using a pencil, managing accessible clothing and remembering where belongings are kept. Children's abilities vary, and some need adjustments to the environment. The goal is not to do everything without help, but to take a growing role in personal routines and belongings.

How to observe without turning home into an exam

  1. Offer a familiar rule-based game. Notice how your child listens, waits for a turn and responds to a mistake.
  2. Read a short story. Ask what happened first and next, without expecting a word-for-word retelling.
  3. Pack a school bag together. Demonstrate the sequence, then hand over one or two steps.
  4. Compare observations with an educator. A preschool teacher or tutor sees the child in a group and can add useful context.
A calm adult prompt

“Let's see what you can already do by yourself and where you still need a hint. We can practise gradually.”

What to avoid

  • Comparing your child with other children approaching school age.
  • Labelling a child “not ready” because of one difficulty or one test result.
  • Replacing play and movement with long sessions at a desk.
  • Expecting the entire first-year curriculum to be mastered in advance.
  • Hiding important observations from the future teacher for fear of judgement.

When to seek additional advice

If a child loses previously acquired skills, has persistent and significant difficulty with speech, movement, communication or daily routines, or the difficulties affect everyday life in different settings, discuss your observations with an educator and a paediatrician or another relevant professional. An article or online quiz cannot provide a diagnosis.

How this topic connects to our lessons

School-preparation lessons can use play to practise communication, understanding instructions, attention, sequences and early learning skills. Activities and difficulty should be chosen after getting to know the child, without promising the same outcome for everyone.
