Attention

How to help a child focus without pressure

A child's attention varies with task difficulty, tiredness, interest and surroundings. Support begins with clear organisation—not criticism.

A practical guide for parents of school-age children

“Concentrate and stop getting distracted” rarely tells a child what to do next. It is more useful to reduce unnecessary demands, identify the first step and make the boundaries of the task visible.

Prepare the workspace

Keep only the materials needed for the current task on the desk. Turn off device notifications and move highly distracting objects away for the moment. Not every child needs complete silence, so judge the quality of the work rather than the habit itself.

Give one clear instruction

Instead of “do all your homework,” begin with a concrete action: open the planner, choose the first subject and read the instruction. Ask the child to repeat the task in their own words to check that it was understood.

Make the stages visible

A short checklist reduces the need to hold the whole plan in working memory. The child can mark each step: read the task, solve two examples, check the answers and prepare materials for the next subject.

Alternate focus and recovery

Choose the length of a work period according to the child's age, the task and how they are feeling—not one universal number. Make the break predictable: stand up, drink water, move a little and return to the next named step.

A calm adult prompt

“We are doing only this task now. What is the first step? After two examples, we will check them and take a short break.”

Use games and everyday activities

Board and card games, puzzles, music, movement sequences and sports require children to hold rules in mind, monitor changes and switch responses. Harvard's Center on the Developing Child describes such activities as ways for 7- to 12-year-olds to practise executive function.

Praise the strategy, not a label

Instead of “you were attentive today,” be specific: “You cleared the desk, checked the instruction and noticed your mistake.” The child then understands which actions helped.

What to avoid

  • Calling the child lazy or inattentive.
  • Making the session longer as punishment.
  • Giving several new instructions at once.
  • Claiming that one exercise can cure attention problems.

When to consult a professional

All children become distracted. If difficulties are persistent across settings and substantially affect learning, relationships or safety, discuss them with the teacher and a qualified medical or psychological professional. Homework behaviour alone is not enough to make a diagnosis.
