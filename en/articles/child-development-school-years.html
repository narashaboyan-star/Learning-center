Child development

How children develop during the school years

A school-age child's development cannot be measured by grades alone. Children are also learning to think, communicate, manage emotions, plan actions and become more independent.

For parents of school-age children

Children develop at different rates. One may expand their vocabulary quickly, another may find logical problems easier, while another needs more support with organisation. Comparing a child with their own earlier progress is usually more helpful than comparing them with classmates.

Thinking and understanding

During the school years, children gradually learn to hold the conditions of a problem in mind, understand cause and effect, consider more than one solution and explain their reasoning. Adults can help with questions such as “How did you understand the task?”, “What do we already know?” and “How could you check the answer?”.

Language and communication

Language continues to develop after school begins. Children become more precise when expressing ideas, retelling information, explaining an opinion, asking clarifying questions and considering another person's perspective. Shared reading and calm conversations give them meaningful practice.

Attention, working memory and self-control

These skills form part of executive function. They help children hold information in mind, filter some distractions, switch between rules and complete a task. Harvard's Center on the Developing Child explains that these abilities develop gradually through practice, interaction and adult support.

Support without taking over

At first, an adult may help make a plan and work through the difficult step. Support can then be reduced: the child marks completed steps, checks the work and learns to correct mistakes independently.

Emotions and relationships

School-age children are learning to cope with setbacks, wait, negotiate, ask for help and recover after conflict. Rather than forbidding an emotion, adults can help name it and choose an acceptable action: pause, make a clear request or return to the conversation later.

Everyday independence

Age-appropriate responsibilities provide practice in planning and ownership. Examples include preparing school materials, organising a workspace, choosing the order of two tasks or managing a short personal checklist.

Ways parents can support development

  • Ask not only about the grade, but what the child understood and found difficult.
  • Offer manageable choices and time for an independent attempt.
  • Play board, language and strategy games.
  • Support movement, sleep, reading and conversation.
  • Increase difficulty gradually rather than expecting adult-level organisation.

When to ask for advice

If a child loses previously acquired skills, has marked and persistent difficulties across several areas, or the situation substantially affects learning, relationships and daily life, discuss your observations with the teacher and a qualified professional. No single checklist fits every child.
