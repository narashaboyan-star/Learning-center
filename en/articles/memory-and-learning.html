Memory and learning

How to help a child remember school material

Good memory for learning is not simply the ability to memorise a text quickly. A child needs to understand the material, focus on it, retrieve it and return to it later.

For parents of students in grades 1–6

Forgetting a rule or a paragraph does not necessarily mean that a child has a “bad memory.” The material may still be unclear, the task may be too large, attention may be depleted, or the child may have reread the page without trying to recall it.

1. Build understanding before memorising

Ask your child to explain the topic in their own words, give an example or solve a similar problem. If that is difficult, return to the unclear step. Meaningful information is easier to connect with what the child already knows.

2. Ask questions instead of relying on rereading

After a short section, close the book and ask two or three specific questions: “What was this paragraph about?”, “What are the three steps?” or “Why did this answer work?” Studies with primary-school children suggest that guided retrieval—with clear questions and support—can be more useful than rereading alone.

3. Return to the material on different days

Several short reviews are usually more manageable than one long evening of study. Revisit the topic after the lesson, the next day and again before an assessment. Each time, ask the child to recall the main idea before checking the book.

4. Break large tasks into smaller parts

A long vocabulary list, text or set of rules is easier to approach in small sections. After each section, pause to name the main point and give an example before moving on.

5. Support sleep, movement and a calm rhythm

Sleep and physical activity are connected with attention, learning and memory. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends 9–12 hours of sleep per 24 hours for children aged 6–12, although individual needs vary. A short walk or active break may also help before study.

A simple review routine

Read a short section → close the book → answer questions → check the answers → explain one example → revisit the topic the next day.

What to avoid

  • Comparing the child with other children.
  • Demanding word-for-word recall before the material is understood.
  • Continuing when the child is clearly exhausted.
  • Promising that one game or technique will create a “perfect memory.”

When to seek additional advice

If difficulties appear suddenly, occur outside learning or significantly affect daily life, discuss them with the teacher and a paediatrician or another qualified professional. This article is not intended to diagnose memory or attention conditions.
