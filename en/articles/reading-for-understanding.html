Reading for meaning

Your child reads the words but misses the meaning: how to build comprehension

Reading a passage aloud smoothly does not tell you everything about how a child understood it. Talk about a short section together: what it says, what remains unclear and where the text offers a clue.

A practical guide for parents of schoolchildren

Find the point where meaning became unclear

Ask your child to point to the place where they lost the thread: an unfamiliar word, a long sentence, a connection between events or the author's main point. This observation helps you choose a useful question. It does not identify a cause or amount to a diagnosis. The What Works Clearinghouse guide for early reading comprehension recommends teaching children to notice when they do not understand and to return to the unclear passage.

Ask to explore, not to test

After a short paragraph, ask “What happened here?” or “What seems to be the main point?”. If your child is unsure, reread it together and find one sentence that helps answer the question.

Read in small, meaningful sections

Before reading, look at the title and wonder what the text might be about. After a short section, invite your child to put its main idea into simple words. For a story, discuss what happened and why a character acted as they did. For an informational text, ask what the author is explaining and which words make it clearer. There is no need to demand a full retelling at once; retelling is a separate topic next in the article queue.

The What Works Clearinghouse guide for grades 4–9 recommends questions about a text, finding the gist of a short section and checking one's own understanding. The guide describes teaching practices; a teacher should choose the length and difficulty of text for a particular child.

Look back at the text for support

After your child answers, ask “Where does it say that?” or “Which words made you think so?”. Some answers are stated directly; others follow from several details. If your child makes a guess without textual support, compare that idea with the passage together. The conversation becomes a search for meaning rather than a hunt for the adult's preferred wording.

The early grades WWC guide also recommends discussing meaning and noticing how a text is organized, such as the order of events or how details support an idea. Its family-friendly reading comprehension tips include asking about important ideas and rereading when meaning is unclear.

Explore an unfamiliar word in context

Choose one word that matters to understanding the passage. Ask your child what nearby sentences suggest it might mean. Then check a dictionary or the teacher's explanation and reread the sentence. You do not need to turn every page into a vocabulary list: the purpose is to understand the passage. The WWC guide for grades 4–9 treats knowledge of words and the topic as part of supporting comprehension.

When to speak with the teacher

If the difficulty recurs across suitable texts, bring concrete examples to the teacher: the passage, what your child could explain and the question where they got stuck. Ask whether the text fits the curriculum and language of instruction, and what support is used in class. Reading pace, task length and expectations for an answer depend on the child, language, grade and school; this article cannot set them. If the teacher notices persistent problems with reading or understanding spoken language, they may suggest an in-person assessment by an appropriate specialist.

How to adapt the advice to a child and school

Discuss the choice of texts and tasks with a reading specialist or primary-school teacher who knows the child's age, curriculum, language of instruction and school rules in Armenia. For persistent difficulties, a teacher and relevant specialist must consider the child's situation directly. A speech-language therapist or another specialist may be appropriate depending on what they observe; this article cannot make that decision. It sets no developmental norms and promises no result from a single exercise.
